Research

One question, seen from multiple sides.

What objects are present in the data, how fast a signal changes, which way gravity points, how far a camera turned, and similar questions about what observed data actually represents, are still largely unanswered in current representation learning. Representations are learned but without a clear identification and separation into individual variables. These questions look like many separate problems needing separate solutions. However, causal representation learning treats them as one, all under the problem of identifiability. Each one asks what a learned representation keeps and what changes it. This could become foundational to how a world model learns world variables.

I study these questions in controlled worlds where the ground truth is known, using synthetic data and frozen video models.

01

Structure by time scale

Can a representation be split into a fast-changing part and a slow-changing part, and the slow part split again?

02

Causes, not only trajectories

Does a video model encode the cause of a motion, or only the path that cause produces?

03

Geometry in frozen models

How much 3D structure survives in a model nobody retrained, and at what scale does it appear?

Iterative-Split-JEPA

Tests whether a model can split a representation into a fast-changing part and a slow-changing part, then split the slow part again, on synthetic worlds where the truth is known.

In progress. One split passes on all 5 seeds. The second split works in clean worlds but misses some of its own pre-set gates, and the repository reports those misses.

Research

V-JEPA causal geometry

A pilot study asking to what degree a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 video model represents the causes of motion (gravity, collisions, friction, etc.), or is confined to the trajectories those causes produce.

Pilot complete, main hypothesis not supported. The gravity direction is more alike across scenes than for raw pixels, but it cannot be told apart from a baseline that tracks the object’s trajectory.

Research

World-Models: Geometric Consistency Probe

Tests whether a frozen V-JEPA 2 video model represents 3D geometry, by fitting linear maps between its representations of rendered scenes before and after a known change.

Mixed. Most small-scale tests fall below their controls. The linear map beats “assume nothing changed” from 10° of camera rotation, and for yaw-only rotation it succeeds with about 1,000 training scenes.

About Me

A short introduction.

I am interested in working on the theoretical foundations of intelligence and self-supervised learning, turning rigorous theory into state-of-the-art engineering systems. My current focus is on world models, causal representation learning and efficient post-LLM training architectures. My most recent work is in recovering individual variables from observational data, organizing them into meaningful geometric structures within representation spaces, and learning efficient interactions between them.

I hold a B.S. from IIT Delhi, studied sociology at the Delhi School of Economics, and worked as an ML engineer before turning to AI research. Along the way I went deep into philosophy and the social sciences, and I often bring perspectives from philosophy and physics to the study of pressing theoretical questions about intelligence.

Through my experiments I have come to believe that studying the geometry of representation space holds a powerful key to unlocking the interventional and counterfactual modes of reasoning. Identifying increasingly abstract individual variables from the observation data, and finding the best geometry in which to represent them and their composable interactions is my current direction of work.

My approach towards the study of machine learning has come to be shaped by my explorations into sociology, philosophy and physics. Three thinkers have particularly shaped how I approach artificial intelligence. Thomas Kuhn showed that scientists work inside a paradigm that shapes which questions and observations count; my choice of research direction and questions is strictly informed by this view. Choosing a question is the most important research choice to make and must come only after we have scanned the other alternative choices and their likely consequences. Intuitions help. But there is no certainty.

  • Jean Baudrillard (Simulacra and Simulation): asked how a copy of the world relates to the real thing. A world model faces the same question: it builds its own version of the world and has to stay faithful to the real one. This is always a pursuit of approximation that can never become exact. Minimizing the difference between approximate and exact is all an intelligent algorithm has to do (e.g. gradient learning, loss functions, backpropagation).
  • Gregory Bateson (Steps to an Ecology of Mind): defined information as "a difference that makes a difference". A causal variable is shockingly close to that idea: at every level it identifies something whose change changes the outcome. A difference of a difference is a powerful mathematical concept in differential calculus too. Here, we probably have a good framework to think through the problems.

Read together, they point to my rather unconventional but very simple view: that intelligence appears as an emergent consequence of motion within an abstract space, which can be referred to as consciousness. And consciousness must be emergent: it must come out of something prior, and in turn be the prior of something else, just like every other phenomenon in the universe. This is, curiously and excitingly, still unknown to humanity.
